Jackal greenhouse simulation · navigation from scratch

How the Jackal drives itself

You type a spot in the greenhouse and press Go. The robot works out a route between the tomato rows, drives it at the speed you chose, and parks on the spot facing the way you asked. This page explains how that works. It assumes no robotics background and no knowledge of the code.

  1. Know the mapThe robot already knows where every plant and wall is. It keeps that knowledge as a grid of 5 cm squares.
  2. Plan a routeA route planner searches the grid for the shortest route that keeps the robot's whole body clear of the plants.
  3. Follow itA path follower steers along the route 20 times a second, always aiming at a point a little way ahead.
  4. Adapt and parkA program called the gateway corrects every command for how this robot really moves on soil, then takes over the last metre to park.

The robot and its world

The robot is a Clearpath Jackal, a four-wheeled research robot about half a metre long. It carries a 1.5 m vertical lift with two cameras that can be raised and lowered to look at plants at different heights. That is what the wider research project is about. For navigation, the lift matters only because it makes the robot taller and slightly larger than the bare Jackal, so it must stay further from the leaves.

The robot lives in a simulated greenhouse, 18 m long and 10 m wide, run by a physics simulator called Gazebo. Inside are four rows of 17 tomato plants, each 1.4 m tall. The rows are 12 m long and 2 m apart, which leaves a 1.3 m clear aisle between neighbouring rows. Open strips at each end, called headlands, let the robot switch from one aisle to another. The soil is slightly bumpy and the wheels can slip, as they would on real ground.

What you give it

The control panel's Navigation box asks for three numbers: X and Y in metres, and Rotation Z, the heading in degrees you want the robot to face when it arrives. Two more settings come from the robot panel: Drive, the cruising speed in metres per second (up to 2.0), and Turn, how hard it may turn (up to 4.0, the same scale the arrow keys use). Press Go to start and Cancel to stop. Touching an arrow key or Stop also cancels the trip and gives you manual control back. The same request can come from a terminal with nav_command.py go --x 4 --y 2 --yaw 180.

The big picture

Think of a delivery driver with a satnav, in a car they have never driven before. The satnav holds a map and picks the roads. The driver's hands keep the car on those roads, moment to moment. Sitting next to them is an instructor who knows this car's habits: the steering is slow, the tyres slip, the brakes are soft. The instructor corrects every input before it reaches the car and parks it at the end.

The robot's navigation has the same parts. Most of them come from Nav2, free navigation software used on many real robots. Nav2 runs on ROS 2, a framework that lets separate programs on a robot send each other messages. The instructor is a program written for this project, called the gateway.

One detail here is unusual. A real robot has to estimate where it is, for example by counting wheel turns. On soil that drifts, because wheels slip. In this simulation the gateway reads the robot's exact position from the physics engine instead. That keeps the navigation honest about where the robot is, but it is also a simplification a real robot could not make (see What it can't do).

Everything happens in a loop

Navigation is a set of loops running at different speeds. Faster loops handle small corrections and slower loops handle the big decisions.

The map: a grid of squares

When the simulation starts, a script builds the map from the simulator's own collision shapes. These are the simple invisible shapes the physics engine uses to decide when things touch. Each tomato plant is a cylinder with a 0.35 m radius, and the walls are thin boxes. The script lays a grid of 5 cm squares over the greenhouse floor and marks every square as free or blocked. Ground steeper than 12° would also be marked blocked. No camera image is involved: the robot is told the map, it does not see it.

How big is the robot?

The same script measures the robot from its 3D model, including the lift at every height and the cameras at every angle. Everything fits inside a rectangle 0.56 m long and 0.48 m wide, which already includes a 2.5 cm safety margin. The planner simplifies that rectangle to the smallest circle that contains it (radius 0.368 m), then adds 2.0 cm of padding, for a planning radius of 0.396 m. The padding covers the gaps between the moments when the path follower checks for collisions.

A trick: grow the obstacles, shrink the robot

Checking a robot-sized circle against every plant at every step is slow. A standard trick avoids it. Grow every obstacle outwards by the robot's radius, and treat the robot as a single point. If the point stays out of the grown obstacles, the real robot stays clear of the real ones.

A price for getting close

A route that grazes the leaves would be legal but unwise. So each free square also gets a price, called its cost, that rises the closer it is to a plant or wall. Right at the edge of the grown obstacles the cost is 252; it falls away quickly with distance and reaches zero 0.6 m from an obstacle. Robotics people call this inflation. The planner adds these prices to the distance it travels, so routes drift to the middle of each aisle where the price is lowest.

When the prices are wrong, the route is wrong

A good example of how much this matters happened while tuning the system. The cost used to reach zero 0.7 m from an obstacle. An inner aisle is 1.3 m wide, so its centre line is 0.65 m from the plants on each side. That is inside 0.7 m, so even the best line through an inner aisle carried a price. The planner treated each inner aisle as if it were 1.8 times its real length. The two outer aisles, between the last row and the side walls, are wider (1.6 m), so their centre lines were free.

The result: asked to drive straight down the centre aisle from the start to (6.5, 0), a 14 m trip, the robot took a 21.7 m detour down an outer aisle, because by the planner's prices the detour was cheaper. The fix makes the inflation distance depend on the layout: it is set just under half the width of the narrowest aisle (0.6 m here). Every aisle's centre line is now free, and the planner chooses between aisles on length alone.

Planning a route

The planner is Nav2's Smac Planner 2D, which uses a classic search method called A* (say "A-star"). It works on the grid of squares like this:

  1. Start at the robot's square. From any square the robot may step to its 8 neighbours. A step costs its length multiplied by 1 + 2 × price / 252, so a step through a free square costs exactly its length and a step next to a plant costs up to three times as much.
  2. Keep a list of squares reached so far. Each one gets a score: the cost of the cheapest way found to reach it, plus the straight-line distance from it to the goal. The straight-line part is an optimistic guess at the cost still to come.
  3. Repeatedly take the square with the lowest score and look at its neighbours. Because of the guess, the search spreads towards the goal instead of in every direction, and it still finds the cheapest route.
  4. When the goal square comes up, trace back the chain of steps that led to it. That chain is the route. A smoothing pass turns its grid zigzags into gentle curves.

The planner runs once per goal. It runs again only if the route becomes blocked or the goal changes; the path follower steers the robot back if it drifts. (It used to replan every second from wherever the robot was. After the robot cut a row-end corner, each new route started inside the bend and hugged the plant a little more, until the robot was too close to plan from at all.) It plans positions only. The heading you asked for at the goal is handled separately, by parking.

Following the route: chasing a carrot

A route is just a line on the map. Something has to turn it into "drive at this speed and turn this hard" twenty times a second. Nav2's Regulated Pure Pursuit path follower does that, and its main idea is old and simple. Imagine dangling a carrot on a stick in front of the robot. The robot picks the point on the route a fixed distance ahead of it, called the lookahead distance, and drives the circular arc that would carry it to that point. A twentieth of a second later it has moved, so it picks a new carrot and a new arc. The result is a smooth curve that keeps pulling the robot back onto the route.

Its rules in this setup:

  • The lookahead is the distance the robot covers in 1.5 seconds at its current speed, kept between 0.6 and 3 m. Slow robot, near carrot. Fast robot, far carrot.
  • If the carrot is more than about 29° (0.5 radians) to one side, the robot stops and turns on the spot to face it before driving on.
  • It slows down on arcs tighter than 2 m radius and as it nears the end of the route.
  • Before every move it projects the robot's outline 1.5 seconds along the planned motion. If that would touch an obstacle, it refuses to move.

The lookahead distance is a trade-off. Try it:

Now remember the rule above: lookahead grows with speed. At the full 2 m/s it stretches to 3 m, which is smooth and steady on a long straight aisle and cuts every row end. That exact problem happened. The fix lives in the gateway, which slows the robot before each bend so the lookahead shrinks by the time it gets there. A second version of the same problem showed up later: at the tightest row end (a U-turn about 0.95 m around the end plant) even the old 1 m minimum lookahead was too long. The robot crept about 15 cm inside the route and ended up too close to the plant to plan from. Now the lookahead can shrink to 0.6 m, and bends are taken slowly enough that 1.5 s of driving stays inside 70% of the bend's radius. That's the next section.

Why this follower? An earlier version used a different Nav2 follower (MPPI), which samples many possible motions and scores them. It settled at about 1 m/s whatever the speed limit was set to. Pure pursuit has an explicit cruising speed, so the panel's Drive setting means what it says.

The gateway: a translator for a robot that slides

The Jackal has no steering wheel. Its two left wheels are driven together and its two right wheels are driven together. To turn, it runs one side faster than the other, and the tyres have to scrape sideways across the ground. This is called skid steering, the same way a tank or a small digger turns. On soil, scraping is unpredictable. These were measured in the simulation:

Ask for thisWhat actually happens
Turn on the spot at 1 rad/s (about 57° a second)It turns at about 0.28 rad/s, roughly a quarter of the request.
Start turning nowThe turn starts about half a second later.
Go from standstill to full speed (2 m/s) at onceThe wheels spin. While they spin the robot can't steer at all: a full turn command produced almost no turn.
Spin half a turn (180°) on the spotIt slides sideways 4 to 24 cm. Faster spins slide less.
Drive straight at cruising speedIt goes about 7% slower than the wheels are turning.

Nav2 assumes the robot does what it is told. This robot doesn't. The gateway sits between them and is the only program allowed to send navigation commands to the wheels. Every command from the path follower passes through these steps, in this order:

  1. Check it. Throw away any command more than 0.35 s old. If the follower ever asks for a speed outside the allowed range, stop and report an error.
  2. Near the goal? Hand over to parking.
  3. Slow down for what's ahead. Before the trip, the gateway works out a safe speed for every point on the route from how sharp the bend is there (keeping sideways acceleration under 0.8 m/s² and turning under 0.47 rad/s, which keeps the lookahead within 70% of the bend's radius), and lets the robot brake at 1.2 m/s² into each slower point. While driving, it applies the lowest of those limits anywhere within the current lookahead distance, so the robot is already slow when the carrot reaches a bend. It also brakes for the goal, but never below 0.15 m/s, the push the robot needs to keep moving.
  4. Don't floor it. Never ask for more than 0.5 m/s above the speed the robot is actually doing, so the wheels keep their grip and the robot can still steer.
  5. Cruise control. Add a small correction, at most 0.2 m/s either way, to hold the planned speed despite slip.
  6. Exaggerate the steering. Divide the wanted turn by 0.28, so the robot is asked for about 3.6 times as much, and add extra in proportion to the gap between the wanted and measured turn to make up for the half-second delay. The 0.28 figure is re-estimated during every trip from steady turns along the route (not while parking), so it adapts if the ground changes.
  7. Respect your limit. Never send a turn command above the panel's Turn setting (at most 4.0, the controller's maximum). Navigation never turns harder than the arrow keys would.

When steps 3 and 4 lower the speed, they lower the turn by the same proportion, so the robot still drives the same arc, only slower.

Parking: the last 70 cm

The planner ignores heading, so the robot often arrives facing the wrong way. Ask for (0, −2) facing 0° when the route arrives from the east, and the robot turns up facing 180°, the opposite way. It has to turn around near the goal. Turning on the spot makes this robot slide, so a turn right at the goal would leave it up to 24 cm off. The gateway handles the finish itself:

  1. Stop short. If the goal heading is more than 29° away from the direction the route arrives in, the gateway brakes so the robot stops before the goal: about 0.7 m before it when the goal faces back the way the route came, closer (down to about 15 cm) when the goal faces sideways, so that the turn never leaves the robot more than 15 cm to the side of where it has to drive. A sideways goal turned 0.7 m early would have to swing across the aisle into the plants.
  2. Take over. It cancels the Nav2 trip. Nav2 would otherwise keep collision-checking motions that are no longer being carried out, and abort.
  3. Turn to the goal heading on the spot, at the full Turn rate, because faster turns slide less.
  4. Drive onto the spot, forwards or in reverse, whichever way the spot lies, using a classic parking controller from robotics textbooks (Siegwart et al., Introduction to Autonomous Mobile Robots). It steers using three numbers: how far away the spot is, the angle to it, and how much the heading still has to change. That removes the sideways slide from the turn with a gentle S-shaped move.
  5. Final small turn if the heading is still slightly off.
  6. Settle and check. Wait until the robot is truly still (under 2 cm/s), then check the measured position is within 7.5 cm and the heading within 4°. Only then is the goal reported as reached.

If the robot arrives already facing roughly the right way, it skips the stop-short and the turn, drives to within 7.5 cm, and finishes with a small turn. While parking, Nav2's collision check is off, so the gateway does its own: if the robot ever gets closer to a plant or wall than its own body radius, it stops with an error.

Staying safe

Several things can command the wheels: navigation, the keyboard, a joystick, and emergency stops. A stock Clearpath component called twist_mux is a priority switch that lets only the highest-priority active source through. Navigation was added at the lowest priority (1). The keyboard is 2, a joystick 10, and safety locks 253 to 255. Pressing an arrow key or Stop takes control immediately and cancels the goal.

A trip goes through these phases, shown on the panel:

Ready→Planning→Navigating→SucceededorCanceledorError

Before a trip starts, the gateway refuses goals that are inside or too close to an obstacle. If the robot itself is too close to a plant to plan from or to turn on the spot, but not touching it, it first creeps straight forwards or backwards (whichever way has more room on the map, at most 30 cm) until it has about 10 cm to spare, then plans. If it still can't plan, it asks you to drive it back into open space. During a trip it stops the robot and reports an error if:

  • position updates are late (older than 0.12 s of simulated time or 1.5 s of real time);
  • any Nav2 program stops running;
  • a command is outside the trip's speed limits;
  • the trip takes longer than 180 simulated seconds or 900 real seconds;
  • the robot gets too close to a plant or wall while parking.

There are no automatic recovery moves, such as backing up blindly when stuck. Near crops, the robot stops and waits for a person. Changing the terrain or wheel-contact settings cancels the trip, waits until the robot has been still for half a simulated second, and rebuilds the map; you press Go again afterwards.

What it can't do

  • It can't see. The map is fixed when the simulation starts. A box or a person in an aisle that is not part of the simulated greenhouse would not be noticed. A real robot would need sensors such as cameras or a laser scanner feeding obstacles into the map.
  • It is told where it is. The position comes straight from the simulator. A real robot must estimate its own position, which robotics calls localisation, and on slippery soil that is a hard problem in its own right.
  • The slip numbers were measured on the default soil. The turning factor adapts by itself during each trip. The others (traction limit, the half-second delay, slide while spinning) would need measuring again for different soil or for a real Jackal.
  • Heading is not planned. A goal that faces against the direction of arrival always costs a stop, a turn and a park, about 10 to 20 simulated seconds. A planner that also plans heading (Nav2's Smac Lattice planner) could avoid that, at a higher computing cost.
  • Very little automatic recovery. The only one is the short straight creep away from a plant when you press Go. Anything harder needs a person to drive the robot back into open space. This is deliberate: no blind motion near crops.

Glossary

A*
A search method that finds the cheapest route through a grid by always exploring the most promising square next.
Cost, inflation
The price the planner pays for passing close to an obstacle. Inflation is the band around each obstacle where that price is above zero.
Costmap
The grid map with a price on every square.
Gazebo
The physics simulator that runs the greenhouse, the robot and its wheels.
Gateway
This project's program between Nav2 and the wheels. It checks and adapts every command, parks the robot and enforces the safety stops. File: tools/navigation_gateway.py.
Headland
The open strip at each end of the rows where the robot can cross between aisles.
Lookahead
How far ahead along the route the path follower aims (the carrot).
Nav2
The standard open-source navigation software for ROS 2. Here it supplies the route planner and the path follower.
Pose
Position plus heading: X, Y and yaw.
Rad/s
Radians per second, a unit of turning speed. 1 rad/s is about 57° per second; a full turn is 6.28 radians.
Regulated Pure Pursuit
Nav2's path follower used here: chase a point a set distance ahead, slowing for tight bends and near the end.
ROS 2
Robot Operating System 2, a framework that lets separate programs on a robot exchange messages.
Simulated time
The simulator's own clock. When the computer can't keep up, simulated time runs slower than real time; timings on this page use it.
Skid steering
Turning by driving the left and right wheels at different speeds, so the tyres scrape sideways. Tanks and small diggers work this way.
Smac Planner 2D
Nav2's grid route planner used here, based on A*.
twist_mux
A priority switch that decides which source of driving commands reaches the wheels. "Twist" is ROS's name for a speed-and-turn command.
Yaw, heading
The direction the robot faces, as an angle measured counterclockwise from the +X axis.
