Greenhouse Jackal Navigation

How the simulated Clearpath Jackal plans and drives to an operator-chosen pose in the greenhouse: a Nav2 Jazzy planner and path follower, wrapped by a gateway that adapts their commands to how a skid-steer base moves on soil.

jackal-greenhouse-sim · Nav2 Jazzy 1.3.13 · Gazebo Harmonic · state as of October 2026

The operator enters a goal pose (X, Y in metres, yaw in degrees, greenhouse frame) and presses Go. The robot plans a collision-free route through the aisles and headlands, turns towards it, drives at the panel's Drive speed on straights, slows for row-end hairpins, and finishes with a docking move that puts it on the goal pose. Everything runs closed-loop on the simulator's measured pose and velocity.

Architecture

Three layers sit between the operator and the wheels. Nav2 decides where to go (planner) and how to steer along the route (path follower). The navigation gateway owns the goal lifecycle and turns Nav2's commands into ones this base can execute. The stock Clearpath control stack (twist_mux, diff_drive_controller) and the Gazebo physics are unchanged.

Why a gateway at all

Nav2's planners and controllers assume a robot that does what it is told: command 1 rad/s, get 1 rad/s. This base does not. On the greenhouse soil a skid-steer turn in place reaches about 28% of the commanded yaw rate, responds about half a second late, and slides sideways while it spins (section Measured base behaviour). The gateway is the single place where those facts live. Nav2 plans and steers in terms of the motion it wants; the gateway converts that into the command the base needs. It is also the only component allowed to publish navigation velocity, so every stop condition is enforced in one place.

Map and footprint

The occupancy map is generated at startup from the greenhouse's actual SDF collision geometry (plants, walls, door frame) at 0.05 m resolution, plus terrain too steep to support the robot (over 12°). Nothing is drawn from rendered images and there is no lidar or SLAM. Roof structure above the robot's full height (1.913 m, lift at full stroke) is excluded.

The footprint is measured from the robot's expanded URDF, including the lift through its whole stroke and the gimbals through their whole range: 0.558 × 0.480 m with a 25 mm margin, circumscribed radius 0.368 m. The path follower's collision check adds 0.020 m of padding, derived below, which gives a 0.396 m planning radius.

Where the 0.020 m padding comes from

Regulated Pure Pursuit checks for collisions by stepping the commanded motion forward one local-costmap cell (0.025 m, half the map's 0.05 m) at a time (for a turn in place, one cell of sweep at the circumscribed radius). Between two checks a footprint corner can move further than the centre when the robot is also turning. Below the in-place-turn threshold, pure-pursuit curvature is at most 2 sin(0.5) / 0.6 m = 1.598 m⁻¹ (0.6 m is the shortest lookahead), so a corner moves at most 0.025 × (1 + 0.368 × 1.598) = 0.040 m per step. Half of that, rounded up, is the padding that keeps the nearest checked footprint within reach of any point the body passes through. The bound is for the commanded motion, not a guarantee against wheel slip. The local costmap used to be 0.05 m, and with the 1.0 m lookahead that needed 0.034 m; shortening the lookahead to 0.6 m would have needed 0.040 m and cost 8 mm of the slack an in-place spin needs in an aisle, so the cell size was halved instead.

Global planner

Smac Planner 2D runs A* on the global costmap's 8-connected grid and smooths the result. Each step costs its length times 1 + m · c / 252, where c is the inflated cost of the cell (0 in open space, 252 next to an obstacle) and m is the cost travel multiplier. Inflation spreads cost outwards from every obstacle: c = 252 · exp(−k (d − r)) for distance d between the planning radius r and the inflation radius, with scaling factor k. So a high multiplier, a wide inflation band and a gentle k all push the path away from plants, and all make it longer. The behaviour tree plans once per goal and replans only if the path becomes invalid or the goal changes. The costmaps hold nothing but the static map, so a periodic replan learns nothing; it used to restart the path at the robot once per second, and after a corner cut each new path hugged the row-end plant more tightly until its start was inside the no-go zone ("Start occupied") and Nav2 aborted the hairpin (5, 2, −180°) from (0, 0).

The long-path problem

The reported "it takes a longer path when a shorter one exists" was real, and it came from this cost term. The search never chose the wrong way around a row. I compared each Smac path against the true shortest path that keeps the 0.416 m planning radius (Dijkstra on the same map with 16-connected moves) for ten start/goal pairs. What cost length was the turn at each row end. With the previous settings (inflation 1.2 m, k = 3, m = 3) paths swung out to the middle of the 3 m headland, about 1.9 m extra per row-end turn.

The wide swing had been added to protect against pure pursuit cutting the inside of hairpins. Shrinking it exposed the real cause of that corner cutting (see Command shaping, stage 3), and once that was fixed the swing could be reduced, first to inflation 0.7 m and then (below) to 0.6 m, with k = 4, m = 2.

The second long-path problem: the outer-aisle detour

A goal at (6.5, 0), straight down the centre aisle from the spawn point, still produced a 21.7 m route down the outer aisle at y = −4.1 instead of the 14.0 m straight run. The cause was the same cost term, this time inside the aisles. An inner aisle is 1.30 m clear, so its centre line sits 0.65 m from the plants on either side, inside the 0.7 m inflation radius. The best line through every inner aisle therefore carried a cost of 99 (of 252), which Smac charges as 1 + 2 · 99 / 252 ≈ 1.79 times its length. A 12 m inner aisle was priced like 21.4 m. The outer aisles, between the last row and the side wall, are 1.65 m clear and their centre line sat outside the inflation band at zero cost. A 7.6 m detour was the cheaper plan.

The fix makes every aisle centre line cost-free. The global inflation radius is now derived at startup from the narrowest clear aisle in the generated layout: one map cell short of half its width (0.6 m for the default 1.30 m aisles, never above 0.6 m). Plants still raise the cost the moment the robot leaves the centre line, so paths stay centred. The choice between aisles is now made on length alone. A unit test checks that the inner-aisle centre lines in the generated map lie outside the inflation radius. On 14 start/goal pairs, run through the real Smac planner on the same map, the mean ratio to the shortest safe path fell from 1.18 to 1.02, and the worst from 1.68 to 1.09. The price is clearance at the tightest row-end corner: 0.16 m beyond the robot's body, where it was 0.23 m.

Path follower: Regulated Pure Pursuit

RPP steers towards a "carrot" point on the path one lookahead ahead of the robot. The lookahead is 1.5 s of measured speed, kept between 0.6 and 3.0 m. The minimum used to be 1.0 m; the tightest row-end U-turn is only 0.95 m in radius, and at the 0.2 to 0.4 m/s the base manages there, a 1.0 m carrot sat so far round the bend that the robot ran up to 0.25 m inside the path and RPP stopped on a collision. If the carrot is more than 0.5 rad off the robot's heading, RPP stops and turns in place first. Otherwise it drives the arc through the carrot at the cruise speed desired_linear_vel, slows on arcs tighter than 2 m radius and near the path's end, and refuses to move if the footprint projected along its command for 1.5 s would hit something.

Why RPP replaced MPPI

The stack previously used Nav2's MPPI controller, capped at 0.22 m/s. Raising MPPI's limit to 2 m/s did not help: its critic balance (path following against the control-cost term gamma) settled at 0.8 to 1.1 m/s whatever vx_max allowed, and six live tuning runs moved it little. MPPI also predicts motion with a model that assumes commanded yaw rates are achieved. RPP has an explicit cruise speed, which is what the operator asked for: Go drives at the panel's Drive value.

Per-goal limits from the panel

On Go, the panel sends its current Drive and Turn values. The gateway writes Drive (at most 2.0 m/s) into RPP's dynamic desired_linear_vel. Turn is a command rate, like the arrow keys, so the gateway sets RPP's in-place turn rate to the actual rate that command produces (Turn × current yaw gain, at most 1.2 rad/s). Navigation therefore never turns harder than the arrow keys would.

Command shaping in the gateway

Every RPP command (20 Hz) passes through the same ordered stages before it reaches twist_mux. RPP's output is treated as the motion it wants, in actual m/s and rad/s.

  1. Validate. Reject stale commands (timestamp older than 0.35 s of sim time) and fail the goal if RPP ever asks for a speed outside 0 to Drive.
  2. Finish (dock or final turn). When the goal faces away from the arrival direction, the speed profile brakes to stop short of it: 0.7 m, or less when the goal is turned sideways, so that turning there leaves the robot at most 0.15 m beside the goal line (d = min(0.7, 0.15 / |sin Δ|) for a turn Δ). A 90° goal docked from 0.7 m had to recover a 0.7 m sideways offset, and the S-move swung about 0.46 m across a 1.3 m aisle into the plants; a 180° reverse dock keeps the full 0.7 m. Within that distance (along the path) and still more than 0.5 rad off the goal heading, or once within 0.075 m of the goal, the gateway takes the rest of the goal over. It cancels the Nav2 goal, because RPP keeps planning and collision-checking motions the gateway is no longer executing and would otherwise abort the goal, and runs the finish from its own 20 Hz loop. It turns in place to the goal heading, then drives onto the goal pose, forward or in reverse, with the polar-coordinate unicycle pose controller (Siegwart et al., Introduction to Autonomous Mobile Robots). With ρ the distance to the goal, α the bearing of the goal relative to the heading and β the remaining heading change, v = 0.5·ρ (0.08 to 0.3 m/s) and ω = 3·α − 1·β (|ω| ≤ 0.8 rad/s). The gains meet the controller's strong-stability condition, so it removes the sideways slide of the turn, including a purely sideways offset, with a short S-move that never swings wider than the starting offset. Forward or reverse is chosen once, when the drive starts. Because RPP's collision check is no longer active, the finish stops with an error if the measured clearance to any collider falls below the robot's physical radius (0.368 m). The gateway then declares success itself, once the measured pose is within tolerance and the base is at rest (below 0.02 m/s and 0.03 rad/s).
  3. Path speed profile. From the planned path the gateway computes a speed limit at every point: curvature measured over ±0.5 m allows 0.8 m/s² lateral acceleration and 0.47 rad/s actual yaw, and a backward pass lets the robot brake at 1.2 m/s² into every slower point ahead. The applied limit is the lowest value within the current pure-pursuit lookahead, because RPP already steers at the carrot. Without that, the robot reached a hairpin fast with the carrot already around the bend and cut straight at the row-end plant. The 0.47 rad/s comes from the lookahead too: it keeps 1.5 s of speed within 0.7 of the curve radius (v ≤ 0.7·R / 1.5), so the carrot stays on the near side of a bend. A separate √(2 · 1.2 · remaining) term brakes for the goal, floored at 0.15 m/s to overcome stiction.
  4. Traction limit. The forward command stays at most 0.5 m/s above the measured speed. A full 2 m/s step otherwise spins the wheels for seconds and the robot loses its steering grip.
  5. Speed feedback. A correction of up to ±0.2 m/s (gain 1) holds the planned speed and recovers the ~7% longitudinal slip at cruise.
  6. Yaw-gain inversion with rate feedback. The wanted yaw rate is divided by the yaw gain (starts at 0.28, refined online from steady turns while following the path; not while docking, where a 90° dock holds the turn command saturated while the base barely pivots and once dragged the gain to its 0.15 floor), with 0.8 × (wanted − measured) added to cover the base's ~0.5 s yaw lag.
  7. Clamp. The result is limited to the panel's Turn command rate (at most the stock 4.0 rad/s) and published on greenhouse/nav_cmd.

Measured base behaviour

Every design choice above traces to a measurement of the simulated base on default firm earth (ground friction 0.9, WheelSlip compliance 0.1 longitudinal / 1.0 lateral). These are properties of the simulation, which was left unchanged. A real Jackal on real soil would need the same measurements repeated.

Goal lifecycle and safety

Before a goal starts, the gateway requires fresh physics feedback, all four Nav2 nodes active and a valid map. The goal and the robot's current position must both be at least the planning radius from any collider; if the robot is closer than 0.10 m outside the planning radius, Go first backs it off. Inside the radius Smac fails with "Start occupied", and just outside it RPP refuses the in-place turn most goals start with: a spin has about 0.20 m of slack off an aisle centre line and slides 4 to 24 cm. The gateway creeps straight forward or backward at 0.15 m/s, toward the side the known map shows more room on, until it has the 0.10 m of room or has moved 0.3 m, then stops and plans if it is at least outside the planning radius. It is refused if it ends closer, below the physical radius, on any manual command, or if its heading runs along the obstacle so that moving gains nothing and it is inside the planning radius.

While the robot navigates, motion stops and the goal fails on: physics feedback older than 0.12 s of simulation time or 1.5 s of wall time; a Nav2 node leaving the active state; a command outside the goal's limits; 180 s of simulated time or 900 s of wall time. Arrow keys, Stop or Cancel cancel immediately and manual commands take over through twist_mux priority. For a terrain/contact Apply the goal is cancelled, the base must stay still for 0.5 s of simulated time, and the map is reloaded; the operator presses Go again.

Success is declared only on the measured pose, at rest: position within 0.075 m and heading within 0.07 rad (4°) of the goal, the goal checker's own tolerances. The gateway finishes goals itself, after cancelling the Nav2 goal for the last metre. Nav2's own goal checker stays non-stateful: a latched "position reached" was observed carrying over into the next goal after a cancelled one, and when the tree still replanned every second, each replan reset it before it helped.

Limits and open questions

  • The yaw gain, lag, traction and slide values were measured on the default simulated soil. The yaw gain adapts online; the others do not. Changing ground friction or slip compliance under Parameters may need them re-measured.
  • A 180° turn in place slides this base by up to 0.24 m. Docking corrects this, at the cost of a slower finish (about 10 to 20 s of simulated time for a goal that faces away from the arrival direction).
  • The map comes from collision geometry. There is no perception of unmodelled or moving obstacles; RPP's collision check only knows the static map.
  • Smac 2D plans positions only, so a goal facing the opposite way to the arrival direction always costs a turn near the goal. A state-lattice planner (Smac Lattice) could plan the heading in, at a higher planning cost.
  • The only recovery is the back-off before planning (at most 0.3 m straight, checked against the known map, only when Go is pressed). Nav2 itself runs no recovery behaviours: no blind motion near crops. A goal-start spin that slides more than ~0.20 m off the aisle centre still ends that goal with an error (RPP: "collision ahead"); the next Go backs off first.
